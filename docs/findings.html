THE FINDINGS, IN EVERYDAY LANGUAGE

Trust goes with support for democracy.
But the story has more than one part.

People who trust representative institutions more usually express stronger agreement that democracy is preferable to other forms of government. That does not mean they always favour checks on leaders, or agree about how political decisions should be made.

Which results does this page describe?

This is a fixed summary of the reference models: the average of trust in parliament, government and parties; separate election-study models; the five-category agreement scale; adjustment for age, gender and education; and the original survey weights where available. The seven outcomes are kept separate. Changing the explorer's settings does not rewrite this summary.

The source contains 55,207 respondents in 29 election studies. These reference models are available for 28 studies. France is absent from these adjusted comparisons because age and education are missing in the supplied file; its unadjusted models are available.

01

The clearest pattern concerns democracy's preferability

In 24 of the 28 available studies, higher trust is associated with more agreement that democracy is always preferable to any other form of government, and the estimate's uncertainty range stays above zero. One study has a clear negative association; in the remaining 3, the range includes both positive and negative possibilities.

The broad pattern also appears when government, parliament and parties are examined separately: respectively 25, 24 and 23 studies have positive associations whose uncertainty ranges stay above zero. This finding is not solely a consequence of averaging the three trust questions.

Still, the estimated size varies. Taiwan has the largest positive estimate in this reference comparison. Great Britain has a smaller positive estimate. Türkiye has a negative estimate: people expressing more trust express less agreement that democracy is always preferable, after the included adjustments. Comparing separate estimates does not, on its own, establish a statistically reliable difference between countries.

02

Supporting democracy can coexist with accepting a rule-bending leader

More trusting respondents also show more agreement that a strong leader may bend the rules in 14 studies, with uncertainty ranges above zero. In 4 studies, the association runs in the opposite direction. In 10, its direction remains uncertain.

These are separate patterns measured in the same surveys. They do not show that every person who supports democracy also accepts rule-bending, or that trust causes either view. They suggest that saying “democracy is preferable” does not capture every part of a person's democratic outlook.

Views about courts tell a less consistent story: 4 studies have clear positive associations, 7 have negative ones, and 17 have ranges that include zero. On women's representation, more trust goes with less agreement that policies have “gone too far” in 17 studies, but with more agreement in 7. The questions should be read individually.

03

Trust is often linked to less appetite for alternatives to elected politicians

The decision-preference questions ask whether important decisions would be better left to business leaders, independent experts, or citizens in referendums, instead of elected politicians.

Higher trust is linked to less support for replacing elected politicians with independent experts in 14 studies, and with citizens in referendums in 17. There are positive associations in 4 and 3 studies respectively. Results for business leaders are more mixed: 9 negative associations, 6 positive ones, and 13 uncertain directions.

A response to these questions is not a complete verdict on democracy. For example, favouring referendums can express a preference for a different form of democratic decision-making.

04

Government ideology does not yet provide a clear explanation

The dashboard estimates cabinet positions by combining expert assessments of parties with the number of cabinet posts they hold. Some cabinet members or parties have no usable ideological position.

For the pre-election comparison, requiring positions for at least 80% of cabinet posts leaves 15 studies. The fitted line suggests a less positive trust–democracy association under cabinets placed further right, but the uncertainty range includes both a negative and a positive relationship. These results do not give a clear answer about government ideology.

This comparison has limited coverage and few countries. It compares patterns between studies, rather than showing how a particular government changes an individual's attitudes. A pre- or post-election cabinet may also differ from the government in office on the interview date.

05

Use the explorer to check how choices change the picture

The default analysis is one reasonable way to examine the question. Visitors can change the trust measure, include fewer or more controls, turn weighting off, or analyse agreement as a yes/no outcome. The Robustness view compares the declared control and weighting choices for a study.

For scale, the Great Britain estimate for democracy's preferability corresponds to about 0.38 points on the original five-point agreement scale when comparing the lowest with the highest possible trust. That is an estimated contrast between the extremes, not a typical person's change, a correlation coefficient, or a percentage-point change in the population.

More controls can also mean fewer respondents with complete answers. A change in the estimate can reflect both adjustment and a different sample. The dashboard reports the sample size for each model.

What these results cannot establish

These are associations from post-election surveys. We cannot tell whether trust changes attitudes, attitudes change trust, or other factors influence both. An uncertainty range that excludes zero is not proof of causation or practical importance. Many comparisons are shown without a correction for looking at many results, and the available survey information does not reconstruct each country's full sampling design. The findings describe this preliminary release and these model choices.
